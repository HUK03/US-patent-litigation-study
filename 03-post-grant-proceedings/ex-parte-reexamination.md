# Ex Parte Reexamination

> **Quick Reference:** Ex parte reexamination has no filing deadline and creates no estoppel for the requester — making it the right tool when the IPR 1-year window has closed, when you want anonymity, or when you have strong prior art that complements (rather than substitutes for) district court invalidity defenses.

---

## 1. What Is Ex Parte Reexamination?

Ex parte reexamination (EPR) is a USPTO proceeding in which any person may request that the USPTO reexamine the patentability of an issued patent based on **prior patents or printed publications**. Unlike IPR, after the request is filed the proceeding is conducted **ex parte** — between the USPTO examiner and the patent owner only. The requester has no further participation.

**Statutory basis:** 35 U.S.C. §§ 302–307

---

## 2. Key Differences: Ex Parte Reexamination vs. IPR

| Feature | Ex Parte Reexamination | IPR |
|---|---|---|
| **Filing deadline** | **None** — can be filed at any time | 1 year from service of complaint |
| **Grounds** | § 102 / § 103 based on patents or printed publications | Same (§ 102 / § 103, patents/publications only) |
| **Institution threshold** | "Substantial new question of patentability" (SNQ) | "Reasonable likelihood" of prevailing |
| **Petitioner participation after filing** | **None** — ex parte; requester has no further role | Yes — full participation throughout |
| **Estoppel on requester** | **None** | § 315(e): estopped from all grounds raised or that could have been raised |
| **Patent owner can add claims?** | **Yes** — patent owner can add new, broader claims | Limited (motion to amend; ~15% success) |
| **Anonymity** | **Yes** — request can be filed anonymously (by a "third party requester" through counsel) | No — must disclose real party in interest |
| **Cost** | $15K–$50K (USPTO fee + counsel) | $75K–$150K+ |
| **Timeline** | 2–4 years (USPTO backlog) | ~18 months from filing to FWD |
| **Effect on district court** | No automatic stay; patent owner can continue to litigate | IPR institution often supports stay motion |
| **Binding effect** | Certificates of unpatentability bind the world | FWD cancellation binds the world |

---

## 3. The Institution Threshold: Substantial New Question of Patentability

To initiate ex parte reexamination, the requester must present a **"substantial new question of patentability"** (SNQ) — meaning the prior art raises a non-trivial question about the patent's validity that was not previously considered by the USPTO examiner.

**What qualifies as an SNQ:**
- A prior art reference that was **not cited during original examination**
- A previously cited reference that was **not fully appreciated** or was misunderstood by the examiner
- A combination of references not previously considered together
- Foreign patent or publication not considered during original examination

**What does NOT qualify:**
- The same prior art already fully considered and rejected during original prosecution
- Rearguing positions the applicant already made to the examiner

**Grant rate:** USPTO grants approximately 90%+ of ex parte reexamination requests — the SNQ threshold is lower than IPR's "reasonable likelihood" standard. However, USPTO grants do not mean the claims will ultimately be cancelled.

---

## 4. When to Use Ex Parte Reexamination vs. IPR

| Scenario | Recommended Tool | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| IPR 1-year bar has passed | **Ex parte reexam** | No deadline; only viable PTAB route for old suits |
| You want to remain anonymous | **Ex parte reexam** | Can be filed anonymously; IPR requires RPI disclosure |
| Prior art is strong but you don't want estoppel risk | **Ex parte reexam** | No requester estoppel at all |
| You want to control the proceeding | **IPR** | Ex parte: you have no role after filing |
| You need a fast result | **IPR** | EPR takes 2–4 years; IPR takes ~18 months |
| You want to pressure patentee to settle | **IPR** | IPR creates more immediate litigation pressure and stay leverage |
| Patent was issued less than 9 months ago, all grounds available | **PGR** | Broader grounds; faster than EPR |
| Complementary to an IPR petition | **Both** (file EPR anonymously to cover different prior art) | EPR creates no estoppel; covers prior art not used in IPR |

## 5. The Anonymity Option — Strategic Use

Ex parte reexamination requests can be filed **anonymously** through patent counsel without disclosing the identity of the requester. This is a significant strategic advantage in two scenarios:

**Scenario A: Competitor doesn't know you are challenging their patent**
Filing a reexamination without revealing your identity avoids signaling to the patent owner that you consider their patent a threat — which could prompt them to file suit against you sooner.

**Scenario B: Avoiding adverse jury inference**
If the case goes to trial, a jury learning you challenged the patent at the USPTO (and the patent survived reexamination) can infer the patent is valid. By filing anonymously, you can challenge the patent without this risk appearing in the trial record.

**Practical note:** Anonymity is maintained only if counsel takes care not to inadvertently disclose the requester's identity in correspondence, argument, or exhibits submitted with the request.

---

## 6. The Critical Downside: Patent Owner Can Broaden Claims

This is the **most important risk** of ex parte reexamination and distinguishes it fundamentally from IPR:

During reexamination, the **patent owner may introduce new or amended claims** — including claims that are **broader** than the original claims, provided the new claims do not introduce new matter. The patent owner can use reexamination to rewrite claims to better cover the accused products.

**Practical implication:** If you file an ex parte reexamination and the examiner issues office actions, the patent owner may respond by amending the claims to more precisely cover your product. You cannot oppose the claim amendments because you are not a party.

**Mitigation:** Before filing, analyze whether the existing claims are the worst version of the patent you will face. If broader claims could be written to better cover your product, weigh the risk carefully before proceeding.

---

## 7. No Automatic Stay of District Court Proceedings

Unlike IPR (where institution provides strong grounds for a stay motion), an ex parte reexamination order **does not automatically support a district court stay**. Courts have discretion to stay proceedings pending EPR, applying the same three-factor test (simplification of issues, stage of litigation, undue prejudice), but:
- EPR's multi-year timeline makes courts reluctant to impose long stays
- The patent owner's ability to add claims during EPR reduces the "simplification of issues" argument
- Most courts treat EPR as insufficient to justify a stay unless the patent is clearly at risk

---

## 8. Practical Role of Ex Parte Reexamination Today

With IPR available and generally superior, ex parte reexamination plays a narrower role:

1. **Post-bar supplemental challenge:** After the IPR 1-year bar has passed, EPR is the only USPTO-based validity challenge available on § 102/103 grounds
2. **Anonymous prior art submission:** File an EPR with strong prior art anonymously as a "torpedo" to disrupt an NPE's ability to assert the patent against other targets — benefiting the entire industry without exposing yourself
3. **Complementary attack:** File an EPR covering prior art that you could not include in your IPR petition (no estoppel concern), running in parallel with the IPR
4. **Low-budget option:** For smaller companies or disputes where IPR costs are prohibitive, EPR is a more economical path

🌏 **Korean Company Note:** For Korean companies sued on patents where the 1-year IPR deadline has passed (common in cases where the patent was asserted years into an ongoing dispute), ex parte reexamination may be the **only remaining USPTO-based invalidity route**. Evaluate it seriously, particularly where strong prior art was not before the original examiner.
